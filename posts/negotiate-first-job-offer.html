How to Ask for More
on Your First Offer

November 14, 2026· 6 min read· by Emma Jane

The offer arrives by phone, usually while you're in a car park or on a break, and the person on the other end says a number and then waits.

Most people say "that sounds great, thank you so much" within four seconds, because saying anything else feels rude and greedy and terrifying all at once. Then they spend two years wondering whether they could have asked.

They usually could have. First offers have room more often than not, and asking once, politely, with a reason, is one of the few genuinely normal things about starting a career.

Short answer: Ask once, after the offer exists and before you accept, with one number and one reason. Base salary moves most often, but start date, sign-on bonus, PTO and an early review are frequently easier yeses.

First, the thing nobody tells you

Your starting salary is the base that every future raise is calculated from. A percentage raise on a bigger number is a bigger number, forever. A few thousand dollars on day one compounds quietly for a decade every time you change jobs, because your next employer asks what you were earning.

That's why this conversation is worth having once. Not because you're being difficult, but because it's the cheapest money you'll ever negotiate.

Before the call: three things to know

  1. The band. Find the range for this role in your city. Job postings, salary sites, and the pay transparency laws in many states all make this easier than it used to be. You're looking for a range, not a precise number.
  2. Your floor. The number below which you'd genuinely walk away, or stay and resent it. Write it down before the conversation, because you'll be tempted to move it mid-call.
  3. Your one-liner. One sentence you can say out loud without rambling. More on that below.

What you don't need is a competing offer. Leverage helps, but honesty helps more, and most employers making an entry-level offer are reading whether you're reasonable, not whether you're in demand.

The moment to ask (and the moment not to)

Ask after a verbal offer exists and before you accept. Not during the interview. Not before the offer, when the only number on the table is your own guess. And not after you've said yes, which reads as a bait and switch no matter how nicely you phrase it.

So when they give you the number, don't accept on the spot and don't decline. Buy yourself a day:

Say this

"Thank you, I'm genuinely excited about this. Can I have until tomorrow to look at the whole package and come back to you?"

Nobody has ever lost an offer for asking for 24 hours. It also gives you time to check the benefits, the commute, and the number in writing instead of doing arithmetic in your head.

The counter: one number, one reason

The counter is a short message with one number and one reason. That's it. Long justifications read as anxiety.

Email script

"Thanks again for the offer, I'd really like to join the team. Based on the scope of the role and the range I'm seeing for similar positions in the area, would you be able to move the base to $X? Everything else in the offer works for me."

Three rules about that number:

  • Ask for one figure, slightly above your target. A range invites them to pick the bottom. Asking for $4,000 more when you'd settle for $2,000 usually gets you something in between.
  • Anchor to the market and the work, not your life. "I have rent to pay" is true and irrelevant to them. "The role includes X and Y" or "similar roles here post a range of..." is a business case.
  • Never claim an offer you don't have. Hiring managers talk to each other, and getting caught ends the conversation permanently.

What actually moves, and what usually doesn't

Usually negotiable Usually fixed
Base salary within the posted band The band itself for an entry-level role
Sign-on bonus, especially if salary is capped Company-wide benefit plans
Start date Standard vacation tiers (sometimes the start date shifts your tier)
Extra PTO, or unpaid leave already planned Probation and notice periods
Remote or hybrid days The job title, at bigger companies
A written six-month review with a raise attached Payroll cycle and pay frequency

That bottom-left one is the underrated move. If the base is genuinely capped by policy, a documented review at six months with a specific number attached is worth more than a $1,000 bump now, and it's easy for a manager to say yes to because it costs nothing today.

If they say no

Sometimes the answer is a flat no, and it's usually about budget bands rather than you. Ask one follow-up question and then let it go:

Say this

"I understand. Could we put a review at six months in writing, with a target for what would need to be true to revisit the number?"

Then get the offer in writing, check that the written number matches what was said on the phone, and accept warmly. A graceful "no problem, I'm still in" leaves you with a manager who remembers you handled it well.

Three ways people get this wrong

  • Negotiating before the offer. Recruiters ask "what are your salary expectations?" early. Deflect once: "I'd rather learn more about the role first, but I'm sure we can find a number that works." If they insist and it's a posted band, giving a number inside the band is fine.
  • Doing it by text. Text feels casual. Offers are business. Call or email.
  • Over-asking on the first job. If you're 23 with no leverage and the offer is already in the market range, a modest ask plus the six-month review is the right play. Asking for 25% more than the band ends conversations and occasionally offers.

FAQ

Isn't it rude to negotiate a first job?

Asking once, politely, with a reason, is standard professional behaviour. Recruiters expect it. What people remember is tone, not the ask.

What if the offer is already good?

Then say so and ask about the six-month review, or the start date, or a sign-on. You don't have to fight for a raise to have the conversation.

Should I tell them what I currently earn?

You don't have to, and in several US states employers can't ask for salary history at all. Redirect to expectations: "I'm targeting the range posted for this role."

How long can I take to decide?

A day or two is normal and expected. A week is fine if you ask for it explicitly. Two weeks of silence reads as disinterest, and they may move on.

What if I already accepted?

Leave it. Renegotiating after acceptance damages trust far more than the money is worth. Take the review at six months seriously instead.

Your next step

Before the offer arrives, not after:

  1. Look up the range for your role and city, write it down.
  2. Write your floor somewhere you'll see it during the call.
  3. Save the "can I have until tomorrow" line in your notes app.
  4. Draft the counter email now, with the number left blank.

Then when the call comes, you're not improvising. You're just filling in a blank, and that's the difference between accepting a number and choosing one.

Once you've accepted, the next money decision is the benefits form that lands in your inbox on day two. Read that one too, because a 401(k) match can be worth more than the raise you just negotiated.

Offer checklist

☐ Range looked up   ☐ Floor written down   ☐ Asked for 24 hours   ☐ One number, one reason   ☐ Six-month review requested   ☐ Offer confirmed in writing
