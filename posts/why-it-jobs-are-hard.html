Why Is It So Hard to Get an IT Job
Right Now? (The Honest Answer)

October 22, 2026·9 min read·by Bloom & Budget

Two hundred applications. Four interviews. One "we've decided to move forward with other candidates, but your profile is impressive." You did everything they told you to do: learned to code, got the degree, earned the certificates, optimized the resume. And the inbox just keeps serving the same polite rejection.

This is the guide we wish someone had sent us before we started doom-scrolling job boards at 1am. No "just network more!" fluff, no judgment — just the honest reasons the IT job market got this hard, how to spot the job posts that were never real, and what actually gets people hired right now.

First: it's not just you

A few years ago, a junior opening got maybe 30 applications. The same posting today gets 300+. That's not because you got worse; it's because everything about the market changed at once. Tech went from a hiring sprint to a hiring diet. Budgets shrank, teams got leaner, and the entry level became the single most crowded place in the entire economy.

So before you read another "nobody wants to work" post on LinkedIn, let's break down what actually happened.

The honest reasons the IT job hunt got brutal

1. Everyone answered the "learn to code" call at the same time

For a decade, the advice was the same everywhere: tech pays well, learn to code. Millions did. Bootcamps boomed, CS enrollment boomed, YouTube degrees boomed. That was genuinely good advice; it just works a little worse when a whole generation follows it simultaneously. The junior end of IT is now the most crowded part of the job market, and you're competing in the biggest crowd in tech history.

2. AI ate the junior task list

Here's the uncomfortable one. The classic entry-level job existed partly because companies needed people to do the simpler work: boilerplate code, basic tests, first drafts, routine tickets, manual checks. That's exactly the work AI tools do fastest. So companies hire fewer juniors, and expect the juniors they do hire to be productive from week one. AI didn't replace developers — it compressed the bottom rung of the ladder.

3. The layoffs pushed experienced people into your queue

The tech layoffs of the last few years didn't just shrink teams; they flooded the market with experienced developers. Someone with five years of experience, laid off through no fault of their own, is applying for the "junior" role you wanted. Companies aren't being cruel by hiring them; they're getting senior skills at a discount. But it means entry-level openings quietly stopped being entry-level.

4. "Entry level" stopped meaning entry level

Read a few postings and you'll see it: "Entry-level developer, 2–3 years experience preferred, familiar with our stack, cloud experience a plus." Experience inflation is real. When employers can pick from hundreds of applicants, they raise the bar just because they can. That's not fair to fresh graduates, and it's also just how oversupplied markets work.

5. Remote work made your competition global

A posting that used to draw candidates from one city now draws candidates from everywhere. Remote hiring connected you to opportunities, sure, but it also connected hundreds of other people to the same opportunities. And on the cost side, many companies found they can offshore entire junior layers. Your competition is no longer the person down the street; it's the planet.

6. Colleges are teaching the recent past

Most IT curricula update slowly, which means a lot of graduates arrive knowing theory and yesterday's tools while employers want today's stack: Git, cloud basics, CI/CD, testing, modern frameworks. This is the good news in the list, actually. It's the one problem you can fix yourself in a few focused months.

7. The hiring funnel itself is broken

Resume-reading robots filter you out for missing a keyword. "Quick apply" buttons vacuum applications into a void. Some postings are ghost jobs — listed to look busy or to build a candidate pipeline, never meant to be filled. You can do everything right and still lose a coin flip you never got to see. Knowing this won't fix it, but it should stop you from taking the silence personally.

The 2019 playbook vs. the 2026 playbook

What worked then What works now
Applications Spray 100 quick-applies 10 tailored ones, research the company first
Proof Certificate of completion Deployed projects with live URLs
Positioning "Full-stack developer" A specific niche with a specific audience
Networking Career fair, LinkedIn connect Referrals and real communities
Income meanwhile Wait for the offer Freelance and small clients as a bridge

If there's one line to remember: the market didn't stop hiring developers. It stopped hiring resumes. The filter isn't your GPA anymore; it's what a hiring manager can click, run, and see.

The fake-job red-flag checklist (screenshot this)

Desperation makes you an easy target, so run every posting through this list before you spend an evening on a cover letter. Two or more red flags: skip.

  • The same listing has been up for months and gets reposted
  • Salary is "competitive," negotiable, or just missing
  • Requirements are buzzword soup ("rockstar ninja, fast-paced environment")
  • The "recruiter" writes from a personal Gmail and rushes you
  • Interviews happen only over Telegram or WhatsApp, never a video call
  • You're asked to pay for training, equipment, or a "background check" first
  • The company has no website, no reviews, no real employees on LinkedIn
  • An unsolicited "job offer" arrives for a role you never applied to

That last one deserves its own sentence: real companies offer you jobs after interviews, not out of the blue. Any "employer" who wants your money before your first paycheck is not an employer.

What actually works now

None of this means give up on IT. It means the entrance moved, so you move with it. Here's the honest playbook for 2026:

  1. Build proof, not certificates. Two or three real projects, deployed, with live links, beat any course certificate. A restaurant site taking real orders or a blog ranking on Google says more about you than a PDF does.
  2. Be publicly findable. A GitHub with real commits, a LinkedIn that shows what you build, maybe short posts about what you learned. Hiring managers do check. Silence reads as "nothing to see."
  3. Niche down. "Developer" competes with hundreds of thousands of people. "I build booking and ordering sites for local restaurants with Python back-ends" competes with dozens, and restaurants can find you.
  4. Chase referrals, not applications. A warm introduction converts many times better than a cold apply. Communities, Discord servers, local meetups, alumni groups; one real conversation beats fifty quick-applies.
  5. Apply like a sniper. Fewer, deeper applications: mirror the posting's language, mention something specific about the company, keep a spreadsheet so you follow up after a week. Also mind the money math while you hunt (more on that below).
  6. Bridge with freelance. Small paid projects keep income coming in, grow the portfolio, and sometimes turn into full-time offers. Waiting tables while you build beats a six-month gap doing only applications.

The money side nobody talks about

Here's where this stops being a career post and becomes a money post, because job hunts are financial events. They now routinely take three to six months. That means:

  • Give yourself a runway. Six months of expenses saved means you can be picky instead of desperate. If you don't have a buffer yet, our realistic emergency fund guide is where to start.
  • Budget the hunt itself. Subscriptions, courses, "premium" job platforms, resume services. They add up fast. Track them like any other expense (our first-salary budget works for first-job hunting too).
  • Never pay to work. Training fees, "placement agency" deposits, equipment purchases. A real employer pays you. Anyone reversing that flow is running the classic mistakes playbook on you.
  • Keep some income while you search. Freelance, part-time, contract work. Income lowers the pressure, and desperation is expensive — it's what makes people sign up for bootcamp #7 or accept the first offer at a company with red flags everywhere.

The Bloom & Budget take

Here's our honest position: the IT job market got harder and that's a fact, not a verdict on you. The door moved from "show credentials" to "show proof," and proof is something you can actually build, starting this week. Ship small things, put them online, tell people about them, and keep your finances boring and buffered while you do it. The people getting hired in this market aren't luckier than you; they're just easier to verify.

And if you're five months deep and feeling like a fraud for not having an offer yet: the average search in this market is measured in months, not weeks. You're not behind. You're in the middle of a slow, crowded process, doing the same thing thousands of eventually-hired people did before you.

A hundred rejections mean you applied. One live project means you built. Guess which one employers can actually see.

While you're hunting

A job search is a budget item. Build your buffer, keep some income flowing, and never pay a "employer" who asks you to. The calmer your money is, the better you negotiate when the offer finally lands.

FAQ: the IT job market

Why is it so hard to get an entry-level IT job?

Three things collided: a record wave of new graduates and career changers, AI tools absorbing the simple tasks juniors were hired for, and layoffs pushing experienced developers back into the applicant pool. Openings still exist, but each one now draws hundreds of applications.

Is AI taking away entry-level IT jobs?

Not replacing developers, but compressing the junior layer. Boilerplate code, basic tests, and routine tickets now get done faster by tools plus one senior person, so companies hire fewer juniors and expect day-one productivity from the ones they hire.

How many applications does it usually take?

In this market, 100–300 applications for a handful of interviews is normal, and searches often run three to six months. Ten tailored applications with referrals or a public portfolio usually beat 300 spammed ones.

Is IT still a good career in 2026?

Yes, but the entrance moved. Software, cloud, security, and data roles still pay well and keep growing. The bottom rung now demands proof of real work instead of certificates of completion.

Do certifications help?

In support, networking, cloud, and security roles, yes, because they're the standard filter. In software development, a portfolio of deployed projects matters much more. A cert plus things people can click beats a cert alone every time.

How do I spot a fake job posting?

Months-old listings, "competitive" salaries, buzzword requirements, Gmail recruiters, Telegram-only interviews, and any request to pay before you start. Two or more: walk away, and definitely read the money section above.
