Why Your First Paycheck Is Smaller
Than Your Salary

Your salary is $48,000 and your paycheck is $3,129. Nobody sat you down and explained the gap, so the first time you saw it you assumed someone made a mistake. Possibly your employer. Possibly you, for not understanding the job you accepted.

Neither. The gap is not an error and it isn't a scam. It's a stack of deductions, some required by law, some chosen by you, and about half of them are things you genuinely want. Once you can name each one, the number stops feeling arbitrary and starts feeling like information.

Here's the whole stack, in the order it comes off, with a worked example you can check against your own stub.

Short answer: Roughly 22% of a first salary goes before it reaches you: federal income tax, 6.2% Social Security, 1.45% Medicare, state tax, plus any health premium and retirement contributions you chose. Budget on net pay, not gross, and expect the first check to be prorated if you started mid-cycle.

Gross pay, net pay, and the space between them

Two numbers matter and they are not the same number.

Gross pay is what you agreed to in the offer letter: your annual salary divided by the number of pay periods. Net pay, which some payroll systems call take-home, is what actually lands in your account. Everything in this post lives in the space between them.

Budget with net pay, always. A budget built on gross pay is a budget that's short by a few hundred dollars every single month, and the shortfall lands on a credit card.

The five things taken out of every paycheck

These come off automatically. You don't choose them, though you can influence two of them.

1. Federal income tax

Withheld based on what you put on your W-4 and what payroll estimates you'll owe for the year. It is a prepayment, not a bill: if too much was withheld you get a refund at tax time, which is your own money returned to you without interest. Too little and you owe in April.

2. Social Security

6.2 percent of your wages, withheld from you, with your employer paying another 6.2 percent that you never see. It funds retirement and disability benefits. There's an annual wage cap where it stops, but it's well above an entry-level salary, so assume it applies to every dollar you earn.

3. Medicare

1.45 percent, no cap, plus an extra 0.9 percent once income passes $200,000 for a single filer. This one is small enough to forget until you add it up over a year.

4. State income tax

Zero in nine states, a few percent in most, higher in a handful. If you moved for the job, this is the deduction that changes most between your old address and your new one, and it's worth knowing before you sign a lease based on a salary figure.

5. Local tax

Some cities and counties levy their own income tax, and a few charge it even if you live elsewhere and commute in. If your stub has a line you don't recognize, look here first.

Quick test: Social Security plus Medicare should equal 7.65 percent of your gross pay, every time, until you hit the wage cap. If that number doesn't match your stub, the problem is with your stub, not with your math.

The deductions you actually chose

These are the ones people forget they signed up for during onboarding week. They're optional on paper and extremely hard to live without in practice, so most of them are worth keeping.

  • Health insurance premium. Your share of the plan, deducted every paycheck. Usually taken before tax, which means it lowers your taxable income rather than just your take-home.
  • Dental and vision. Often a few dollars each. Cheap enough to ignore, useful the first time you need a filling.
  • 401(k) contribution. Whatever percentage you elected. Pre-tax in a traditional account, so a 5 percent contribution costs you less than 5 percent of your take-home.
  • HSA or FSA. Health savings and flexible spending accounts. The HSA is the better deal: it rolls over, it's yours when you leave, and it's tax-free in, out and in between.
  • Life insurance, disability, union dues, commuter benefits. Small lines that add up. All of them can be changed during open enrollment, and most can be dropped if money is genuinely tight.

The pre-tax versus post-tax distinction is worth ten seconds of attention, because it changes your real cost. A $100 pre-tax deduction lowers your take-home by about $100 minus your marginal tax rate, while a $100 post-tax deduction lowers it by the full $100. Your pay stub usually groups them, but not always. Read the benefits post if those boxes felt like a foreign language in week one.

A worked example: $48,000 a year

Same person as our first salary budget: 24 years old, one person, no kids, single filer in a state with a modest income tax, on the employer's basic health plan. Paid monthly, which keeps the arithmetic honest.

Two caveats, because your stub will differ. If you contribute to a 401(k), that comes off before the tax lines shown here, which is why the taxable amount on your stub might be lower than your gross. And if you live somewhere with no state income tax, your take-home is roughly $110 a month higher than this example, which is $1,320 a year, which is a real vacation.

Why your first check is the weirdest one

Your first paycheck is often a different size than the rest, and it's worth knowing why before you panic.

  • It's usually prorated. If you started on the 14th, you're paid for half a period, not a whole one.
  • Benefits sometimes start on the first of the following month. So check one has no premium deducted and check two does, which makes it feel like your pay went down.
  • Some employers take a full month of premium on the first check to get ahead of the billing cycle, which makes the first one smaller and the rest normal.
  • A signing bonus is withheld like a bonus, often at a flat 22 percent federal, not at your normal rate. That's why a $2,000 bonus feels like it arrives with a haircut.

None of that is an error. But if check one and check three are different sizes and nothing obvious explains it, ask payroll. It's a normal question and they answer it weekly.

What you can actually change

Most of the stack is fixed. Three levers are yours.

Your W-4. More allowances or a higher dependent credit means less withheld per check and more money now, with the risk of owing later. For a first job with one income, the default usually lands close to right. If you got a giant refund last year, you gave the government an interest-free loan and can fix that with a new W-4.

Your 401(k) percentage. Raising it lowers take-home and lowers taxes at the same time. A 1 percent bump costs a first-salary worker roughly $20 to $40 a month in take-home and buys a surprising amount thirty years later. Get the full employer match before anything else, because a match is an instant return nothing else in your financial life will beat.

HSA over FSA where you have the choice. Same tax treatment going in, but the HSA is yours forever and the FSA generally vanishes at year end. If you're choosing during enrollment and you're healthy, the HSA is the safer bet.

Everything else, FICA most of all, is not negotiable. Any service promising to reduce Social Security withholding is selling you something that doesn't exist.

How to read your pay stub in two minutes

Six things, in this order:

  1. Pay period. Check the dates match the work you did. Most payroll mistakes are date mistakes.
  2. Hours or salary basis. Are you paid for the time you actually worked, including overtime if you're non-exempt?
  3. Gross pay. Compare it to your offer divided by the number of periods. It should match unless this is a prorated check.
  4. Taxes. Federal, Social Security, Medicare, state, local. Look for anything unfamiliar and ask.
  5. Deductions. Premium, retirement, HSA. Confirm nothing is missing that you enrolled in, and nothing is still there that you cancelled.
  6. Year to date. This is the column people ignore and later need. It's how you check your W-2 in January.

Keep the stubs. If you ever need to prove income for an apartment, a loan or a visa, a year of pay stubs answers the question faster than anything else.

What to do if the number is wrong

Email payroll, don't guess, and be specific. Something like: "Hi, my check dated the 15th shows $2,240 gross for a full pay period, but my salary basis is $4,000 monthly. Can you confirm whether this check was prorated? Happy to send my offer letter." You're not accusing anyone. You're asking a factual question in writing, which is the version that gets fixed.

Two things make this go faster: send it the day you notice, and put the pay period dates in the subject line. If it's a deduction you didn't sign up for, ask for the enrollment record. If it's hours, check your own timekeeping first. Half of payroll disputes are resolved by the employee finding their own typo, and that's a fine outcome too.

Your salary is a promise. Your take-home is the number you can actually spend.

The number to take to every other decision

Net pay is the input for everything else, and getting it wrong is what makes budgets feel impossible rather than tight.

Use it for rent: a landlord looking for 40 times the rent is thinking in gross, but you should be thinking in take-home, because that's what pays it. Use it for the four walls in the first-salary budget, for the move-out number in what moving out actually costs, and for any savings target from the emergency fund. All three of those posts assume a take-home figure for a reason.

One habit, and it's the whole post: every time you're given a salary number, divide by twelve, subtract about 25 percent, and work backward from that. You'll be closer to reality than anyone who budgets on the offer letter.

FAQ

Why is my first paycheck so much smaller than I expected?

Usually prorating: you were paid for a partial period because you started mid-cycle. Sometimes it's a full month of health premium taken up front, or a benefit starting on the first of the next month. Compare the pay period dates on the stub to your start date before assuming anything is wrong.

How much of my salary should I expect to take home?

For a single person on an entry-level salary in the US, 73 to 80 percent of gross is the normal range, depending on your state, your health premium and whether you contribute to a retirement account. Twenty-two percent gone is typical, not unusual.

Is it better to have less tax withheld so my check is bigger?

Only if you'd otherwise get a large refund. Withholding is a prepayment, so less withheld means a bigger check now and a smaller refund, or a bill, in April. If your budget is tight, adjusting the W-4 can help, as long as you set the money aside for tax time rather than spending the whole difference.

Can my employer take taxes out of my bonus at a different rate?

Yes. Bonuses and supplemental pay are often withheld at a flat 22 percent federal, separate from your normal rate. Your actual tax liability is still calculated on the year's total income at tax time, so a bonus can end up owing a little extra or returning a little more.

Do I pay Social Security on every dollar I earn?

Up to an annual wage cap, which adjusts most years and is far above an entry-level salary, so assume yes. Medicare has no cap, and an additional 0.9 percent applies once income passes $200,000 for a single filer.

Why did my paycheck drop after the first month?

Almost always a benefit starting. Many plans begin the first of the month following your hire date, so check one has no premium and check two does. Your gross pay stayed the same; the deductions changed. Read your enrollment confirmation to confirm the amount matches.

Your next step

Open your most recent pay stub and find three numbers: gross pay, total taxes, and net pay. Write them down.

  1. Divide total taxes by gross. That's your effective tax rate. Anything between 15 and 20 percent on a first salary is normal.
  2. Add up the deductions that aren't taxes. That's what your choices cost per period, and what you can change.
  3. Take the net number to your budget. Not the salary, not the gross figure from the offer letter. The net.

Ten minutes, and the number you plan your life around finally matches the number in your account.
