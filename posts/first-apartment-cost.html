What Moving Out Actually Costs
(Before You Own a Single Thing)

Nobody warns you about the week before the keys. You've done the math on rent, you know what the apartment costs per month, and you feel ready. Then the leasing office sends the move-in total and it's a number you have never once held in your account at the same time, and the whole plan tilts sideways.

That's not a sign you can't afford to move out. It's a sign that rent is the smallest part of the first month. Moving out is cheaper than people fear in the long run and more expensive than anyone expects on day one, and both of those are true at once.

So here's the honest bill, line by line, with the stuff that never makes it onto a Pinterest checklist.

Short answer: Expect three to four months of rent in cash before you sleep there: deposit, first month, application fee, utility deposits and roughly $550 to $1,380 to set up an empty room. On a $1,150 apartment that is about $2,900 at the barest level and $3,700 with normal furniture.

The move-in stack: what you pay before you sleep there

The deposit is the number everyone plans for. It's rarely the biggest one.

What the landlord collects

  • Application fee. Usually $30 to $75 per adult, and it's gone whether or not you get the place.
  • Security deposit. Often one month's rent, sometimes less, more if you have a pet. Some states cap it, some don't.
  • First month's rent. Due at signing, before you've moved a single box.
  • Last month's rent. In competitive cities some landlords ask for it up front too. Ask before you fall in love with the place.
  • Broker or holding fee. Only in some markets, but in those markets it can be a full month's rent.

There are also deposit alternatives now, where a company guarantees your deposit for a small monthly fee instead of you paying it all at once. They're legitimate and they can be a real bridge. Read the terms, because the fee usually keeps running for as long as you rent, and some of them don't return anything at move-out.

What the utility companies collect

Electric, gas, water and internet all want to know you're good for it. If you have no credit history, or a thin file, expect a deposit on one or two of them, often $50 to $200, returned after a year of on-time payments. Some companies skip the deposit entirely if you ask. It costs nothing to ask.

The part nobody puts in a spreadsheet

You're moving into an empty room. Even if you own a bed and a frying pan already, there's a gap between what you have and what a functioning home requires, and you pay for that gap in cash during the first three weeks. The next section is what it actually costs.

The test that saves people: before you sign anything, ask yourself whether you could cover next month's four walls if you lost the job in week two. If the answer is no, the apartment is affordable on paper and unaffordable in practice.

That "barest" column is not a worse life, by the way. A mattress, a pot, a plate and a lamp covers 80% of what a first apartment is for. Everything above it is comfort you can add one paycheck at a time, and adding it slowly is how people end up with a home they actually like instead of a credit card balance they dread.

The "$550" line assumes you buy secondhand and accept a few free things. Marketplaces are full of people who bought a desk for a job they left. Buy-nothing groups give furniture away when someone moves. End of month is the best week to look, because that's when leases turn over and people are desperate to not haul a dresser down three flights.

The whole number, for a $1,150 apartment

Same person as our first salary budget: $48,000 a year, $3,129 a month take-home, rent at $1,150. Here's the cash she needs on day one.

At the barest level, buying almost everything used and taking the free desk, the same apartment costs about $2,900. That's the realistic range: three to four months of rent in cash, and only $1,150 of it goes to the landlord as rent.

This is why the advice "have first and last month saved" fails people. It's not wrong, it's just incomplete. The deposit and first month are the part with paperwork. The setup money is the part that quietly goes on a credit card.

What rent can actually afford, not what feels exciting

Two numbers to run before you tour anything.

Thirty percent of take-home. On $3,129 that's $939 for rent and utilities together. In most US cities that number will feel impossibly low, and you're allowed to break the rule. Just know what you're trading when you do.

Forty times the rent. Many landlords screen on gross income of about 40 times the monthly rent. For a $1,150 apartment that's $46,000 a year. Below that, expect to need a co-signer or a bigger deposit, and ask before you apply so the application fee isn't wasted.

If rent comes in above 30%, the money has to come from somewhere. Move it consciously: take it out of fun money and transport first, then from the "nicer" column in the setup table. What you should not do is take it out of savings entirely. Even $50 a month keeps the habit alive, and the habit is the thing that makes the next few years easier.

Rent is a number you agree to every month. Moving costs are a number you agree to once, while you're excited and not thinking clearly.

The costs that show up in month two

The first month is the expensive one. The second month is where people discover the budget was fiction.

  • Electric and gas. $90 to $170 depending on climate and how old the building is. A summer with a window AC unit is not the same bill as October.
  • Internet. $45 to $70, and the intro price usually expires after a year.
  • Renter's insurance. $12 to $20 a month, and most leases require it. It covers your things and, more importantly, your liability if you flood the unit below.
  • Laundry. If there's no machine in the unit, budget $30 to $50 a month. It's the most underestimated line in apartment life.
  • Food for one. $320 to $400 is normal. Feeding one person costs more per head than feeding a family, because there's no bulk discount and no one to split a takeout order with.
  • The starter tax. Salt, oil, spices, foil, toilet paper, a mop, a plunger, a spare lightbulb. Roughly $60 a month for the first two or three months, then it settles.

Running the $1,150 apartment through the four walls: rent $1,150, utilities $140, internet $60, groceries $360, laundry $40, transit $165, loan minimum $190. That's $2,105 of a $3,129 paycheck, which leaves $1,024 for savings and life. It's tight. It's also survivable, and that's the standard to hold yourself to in year one.

Furnishing it without a payment plan

Once the boring math is done, this is the fun part, and it's where a lot of people make a decision they regret for two years.

Rent-to-own is the most expensive furniture in America. A $900 sofa on a weekly payment plan commonly totals two to three times the sticker price once the term ends, and you don't own it until the last payment. If the plan is the only way to furnish the apartment, the honest move is a secondhand sofa and a $40 throw.

Store credit cards are the second trap. The "12 months same as cash" offer on a furniture card usually accrues interest retroactively from day one, so paying $50 short in month twelve means paying interest on the entire purchase. If you can't clear it in full, it isn't free.

Then there's the free version. A room looks intentional with three things: a light that isn't the ceiling fixture, something soft underfoot, and one plant. A thrifted lamp, a rug from a marketplace, and a cutting from a friend's pothos cost under $60 and do more for a room than $600 of matching decor. Frames from a thrift store with printed photos are the cheapest gallery wall there is.

Give yourself a 30-day rule on any decor purchase over $40. If you still want it in a month, and the money exists, buy it. Half the time you'll have stopped wanting it, and the room will have grown on you exactly as it is.

Where people lose money moving out

  • Signing without reading the deposit terms. "Non-refundable cleaning fee" and "security deposit" are different things. Ask what you get back and what you have to do to get it.
  • Not photographing the apartment on move-in day. Timestamped photos of every scratch and stain are the entire defense for your deposit a year later. Twenty minutes now, hundreds of dollars later.
  • Moving in with a partner or friend on a verbal agreement. Write down who pays what and what happens if someone leaves. Awkward for ten minutes, protective for a year.
  • Putting the deposit on a credit card. Sometimes unavoidable. Pay it off within the first statement, and know it can spike your credit utilization right when a landlord is checking it. See how utilization works before you swipe.
  • Forgetting the commute changed. A cheaper apartment two zones out can cost more than the rent it saved, and it costs you an hour a day that you never get back.
  • Choosing the benefits form later. If you're moving for a new job, the health plan and 401(k) decisions happen in week one and change your take-home for the whole year. That's in the benefits post, and it's worth ten minutes before you sign a lease on a number you haven't recalculated.

How to save the move-out number

Work backwards from the total, not from a vague goal. If you're aiming at a $1,150 apartment and the $2,900 barest version, you need $2,900 plus one month of walls as a buffer, so roughly $5,000 in cash. That sounds enormous until it's a monthly line item.

At $626 a month, the automatic savings figure from the salary post, that's eight months. At $300 a month it's about seventeen. Both are fine. What breaks people is starting four months before the lease ends and putting the shortfall on a card.

Keep it somewhere separate and boring, the same way you'd build an emergency fund. A moving fund that lives in your checking account becomes a going-out fund by March, and then a money mistake with a lease attached.

FAQ

How much should I have saved before moving out?

Three to four months of rent in cash: the deposit, the first month, setup costs and a small buffer. For a $1,150 apartment that's roughly $2,900 at the barest level and $3,700 at a normal one. Anything less works only if someone else is covering part of the setup.

Can I move out with no savings at all?

Sometimes, and it's risky in a specific way. A furnished sublet, a room in a shared house, or a deposit-alternative service can get you in the door for a few hundred dollars. The danger isn't the move, it's month two, when the first unexpected cost arrives with no cushion and becomes a credit card balance. If you go anyway, keep the first three months as frugal as you can stand and rebuild the buffer fast.

Is the security deposit or the first month's rent due first?

Usually both at signing, in the same payment. The application fee comes earlier and is non-refundable. Ask up front whether last month's rent is also required, because in tight markets it often is.

Do I actually need renter's insurance?

Most leases require it and it usually costs $12 to $20 a month. It covers your belongings and your liability, which matters more than people think. If you accidentally leave the tub running and damage the unit below, your landlord's insurance pursues you, not their policy.

Is it cheaper to rent furniture or buy it used?

Buy used, every time. Rent-to-own commonly totals two to three times the sticker price over the term and you don't own anything until the final payment. Secondhand furniture plus a $40 throw and a thrifted lamp gets you a room that looks intentional for a fraction of one payment.

Should I move out before my first paycheck?

Only if the savings already cover the full move-in stack. A lease signed on a promise of income is the riskiest version of this, because the first month demands more cash than any month after it. Wait one pay cycle if you can, then move knowing the number is real.

Your next step

Before you tour another apartment, do one unglamorous thing: write the real number down.

  1. Pick the rent you're actually considering, not the one you hope to find.
  2. Double it for the deposit and first month, then add $50 for the application and $1,380 for a normal setup, or $550 if you're doing it secondhand.
  3. Compare that total to what's in your savings today. That gap is the only number that decides your timeline.
  4. Divide the gap by what you can move to savings each month. That's how many months until you can sign without borrowing.

It's less romantic than picking paint colors. It's also the reason some people move out calm and others spend the first year paying off a couch.
