Small Balcony Decor on a Budget:
What It Actually Costs

The balcony is the cheapest room you own and the easiest one to overspend on. It's also the only square metre of the apartment that is mostly yours, which is why it's the first place people start browsing outdoor furniture at 11pm and the last place they check the lease.

Here's the honest version: a balcony that looks like the photos costs less than you think if you buy slowly, and more than you think if you buy all at once. So this post gives you three real versions, the exact line items, and the lease rules that decide whether you get your deposit back.

And if you came here looking for small balcony decor ideas, the honest version is that the ideas are the cheap part. It is the shopping that costs you, so the numbers come first and the ideas follow.

One thing before the numbers: nothing here requires drilling, mounting or a landlord's permission. Everything is freestanding, which is both the renter-friendly choice and the cheaper one.

Short answer: A balcony costs $0 to $362 depending on patience. The realistic middle is about $152: a secondhand bistro set, two planters, two plants, warm string lights and a mat. Buy in that order, check the lease for mounting and grill rules first, and never finance outdoor furniture.

What a balcony actually needs

Five things, in this order of impact. Most people buy them backwards, starting with decor and ending with seating.

  1. Somewhere to sit. One chair is enough. Two makes it a room.
  2. Something at eye level that isn't concrete. Plants, or a screen, or both.
  3. Light you control. A string of warm bulbs beats the ceiling fixture every time.
  4. Something underfoot. A mat or a small rug, so it reads as a room instead of a ledge.
  5. A surface. A small table for a cup, a book, a plant you're watering.

That's the whole list. Anything beyond it is nice, and none of it is required.

The free version isn't a compromise

Some of the best balconies I've seen cost nothing, and they cost nothing because the person was patient rather than clever.

  • Buy-nothing groups. Search your neighbourhood group for "patio", "balcony", "chairs" once a week. Outdoor furniture moves constantly.
  • End of month on marketplaces. That's when leases end and people would rather give a chair away than carry it down three flights.
  • Cuttings. Pothos, spider plants and mint all root in a glass of water. A friend with a plant is a free nursery.
  • The curb on moving day. In any building with a turnover week, the alley does half your shopping.
  • What you already own. A kitchen chair, a side table, a throw blanket. Nobody can tell the difference from four feet away.

The only thing I'd spend money on in the free version is a $12 mat, because it's the item that changes how the space reads more than anything else on the list.

The lease rules that decide your deposit

This is the part that costs more than furniture. Read your lease for these five things before anything goes outside.

  • Holes and mounting. Most leases forbid drilling into walls or railings, and hanging planters are a classic deposit deduction. Use tension rods, adhesive hooks rated for outdoor use, or freestanding shepherds hooks instead.
  • Grills and open flame. Many buildings ban gas and charcoal grills on balconies outright, and some ban candles. Fire codes are stricter than landlords, so ask.
  • Weight and railings. Don't hang planters or lights from a railing that was never rated for it, and don't stack heavy pots against glass panels.
  • Water and drainage. Pots need saucers, and saucers need to not overflow onto the neighbour below. Standing water is how balconies turn into damage claims.
  • Storage and clutter. Many leases ban storing bikes, boxes or anything visible from outside. A weatherproof box is the workaround, not a pile under a tarp.

Two minutes of reading, one photo of the empty balcony on move-in day, and you've protected several hundred dollars. That photo habit is in the move-out cost post too, because it's the same deposit.

The $0 question: if the lease bans grills and mounting, the balcony is a reading-and-plants room, not a cooking room. Deciding that first saves you from buying a grill you can't use.

What survives weather, and what doesn't

Cheap outdoor furniture fails in a predictable order: fabric first, then untreated wood, then metal joints.

  • Buy metal or resin, skip untreated wood. Powder-coated steel and resin survive rain and sun. Anything sold as "acacia" needs oiling or it greys and splits.
  • Cushions come inside. Even "outdoor" fabric mildews if it stays wet. A $18 storage box solves this and keeps the balcony tidy.
  • Terracotta over plastic. It's heavier, which is good in wind, and it doesn't go brittle in sun. Plastic pots crack in two summers.
  • Plants that forgive neglect. Herbs, succulents and trailing pothos handle a balcony better than anything delicate. Ask at a local nursery what survives your light, because a north-facing balcony is a different climate.
  • Lights rated for outdoors. Indoor string lights are the most common balcony purchase that dies in a month.

What never to finance

Outdoor furniture is one of the most aggressively financed categories in retail, and it's a bad trade every time.

Rent-to-own patio sets commonly total two to three times the sticker price over the term, and you don't own the set until the final payment. For something that lives outside and gets rained on, that's a lot of interest on an object that will fade.

Store credit cards with "12 months same as cash" accrue interest retroactively from day one if you miss the payoff by a dollar. If you can't clear it in full, it isn't free.

Buy-now-pay-later turns a $140 set into four decisions and one more thing to track. On a balcony, the honest move is the $80 secondhand set and a $12 mat until you have the rest in cash.

This is the same principle as the money mistakes post: the object is rarely the problem, the payment plan is.

The $20 that makes it look finished

If the balcony feels unfinished and you have twenty dollars, spend it in this order:

  1. A mat at the door. It separates inside from outside and instantly reads as intentional.
  2. One warm light source. A single string of bulbs at 2700K, draped along the railing, is the difference between a ledge and a place to sit.
  3. One plant at eye level. Height matters more than quantity. A pot on a box does the job a plant stand would.

Three moves, twenty dollars, and the space stops looking like an empty rectangle.

How to save for it without noticing

The mistake is buying the balcony in one weekend on a card. The fix is a sinking fund, which is just a savings target with a date attached.

Pick the $362 version, divide by three months, and move $121 into a separate account on payday. In three months you buy it in cash, in the right order, and nothing arrives that you regret. If $121 is too much this quarter, do the $152 version over two months at $76, and upgrade the rug next spring.

The same logic as an emergency fund, aimed at something you'll actually enjoy looking at. It works because the decision was made once, in advance, rather than in a checkout flow at midnight.

A balcony is the cheapest way to add a room to an apartment you don't own.

FAQ

How much should I budget for a balcony makeover?

Between $0 and $400 depending on patience. The realistic sweet spot is about $150: a secondhand bistro set, two planters, two plants, string lights and a mat. Anything above that buys durability and finish, not a different look.

Can I hang things without drilling?

Usually yes, with tension rods, outdoor-rated adhesive hooks, or freestanding shepherd hooks for planters. Check the lease first, because mounting is one of the most common deposit deductions, and never hang weight from a railing that wasn't rated for it.

Are string lights allowed on a rented balcony?

Almost always, if they're outdoor-rated and attached with clips rather than nails. The risk isn't the lights, it's the fasteners and any extension cord running through a door that can't close properly.

What plants survive on a balcony?

Herbs, succulents and trailing pothos are the forgiving choices, and terracotta pots handle sun better than plastic. The deciding factor is light direction: a north-facing balcony needs shade-tolerant plants, so ask a local nursery rather than trusting a generic list.

Is it cheaper to buy a set or collect pieces?

Collecting pieces is cheaper and looks less catalog-like, but takes months. A secondhand set plus two separate chairs is usually the best of both: one purchase gets you seated, and the extras arrive when they turn up.

Do I need to tell my landlord about balcony plants?

Rarely, but do check for rules about weight, drainage and anything visible from outside. Saucers under every pot are the difference between a plant collection and a water damage claim against your deposit.

Your next step

Before you buy anything, do this in order.

  1. Read the lease section on balconies and patios. Note what's banned: grills, mounting, storage.
  2. Measure the space, then take one photo of it empty. The photo is your deposit evidence and your before shot.
  3. Decide which version you're doing: free, $152, or $362. Then open a marketplace search and wait a week.
  4. Buy the mat first. It's $12 and it changes the room more than the furniture does.

A week of patience is worth more here than any shopping skill, and the balcony will still be there when the right chair turns up for $40.
