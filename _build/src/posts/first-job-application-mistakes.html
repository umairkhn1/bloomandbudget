Most first-job advice is about being impressive. The mistakes that actually cost you money are quieter than that: the ones that get your application filtered out before a person reads it, or that leave a few thousand dollars a year on the table before your first week is over.

On the salary used across this site, $48,000 a year and $3,129 a month take-home, a bad application is not a small thing. It is weeks of no income, or a starting number that follows you for a decade.

Here are the seven that cost the most, what each one is worth, and the fix. None of them require you to be a different person.

1. Sending the same CV to forty places

The spray-and-pray approach feels productive because it is measurable. Forty applications in a weekend. But a generic CV answers a question nobody asked, and it is the first thing a hiring manager notices.

The fix is smaller than people expect. You do not rewrite the whole document. You rewrite the top third: the summary line and the three bullets closest to the job. If the posting says "reconcile accounts" and your CV says "handled finances," you lose to the person who used their words.

Twenty tailored applications will usually beat two hundred identical ones, and the tailored version takes about twelve minutes each once you have done three.

2. Applying with no human attached

A portal submission goes into a stack. A submission with a name next to it goes into a shorter stack. That is the whole advantage of a referral, and it is not reserved for people with connected parents.

Find one person at the company who does the job you want, ideally a junior rather than the manager, and send a short note: who you are, the role, one specific reason you are writing to them. Not a paragraph about your passion. Two sentences and a question.

If nobody answers, apply anyway. You have lost ten minutes. If you are finding that nobody answers at all, the honest read on the market is in why it is so hard to get an IT job right now.

3. Naming a salary number first

This is the most expensive mistake on the list and it happens in a form field, in one line, usually because the field will not let you continue.

Whatever number you type becomes the anchor for everything after it. Start $2,000 low and you are not $2,000 behind. You are $2,000 behind every year, and every raise you get is a percentage of the lower base. Ten years of that is well over $25,000, for a number you guessed in ninety seconds.

When you can, answer with a range based on the market for that title in that city, and put the bottom of the range where you would actually say yes. When the field is mandatory and you truly have no information, use a range and add "negotiable depending on the full package" in the notes box. The mechanics of the conversation itself are in how to ask for more on your first offer.

4. Waiting until you feel ready

Almost nobody feels ready. The people who get hired applied at about eighty percent and learned the rest in the job.

The cost here is easy to calculate and easy to ignore. At $3,129 a month take-home, a month of searching is a month of not earning: roughly $720 a week. Two weeks of polishing a CV that was already fine is $1,440 gone.

Send the first three applications this week, imperfect, and treat the responses as free information about what your CV is missing.

5. An email address that undercuts you

It should not matter. It does. An address from 2013, a nickname nobody can spell, a shared family inbox with someone else's name on it: all of it reads as careless before anyone gets to your experience.

Make a free address that is your first and last name and nothing else. Then check three things most people skip: that your voicemail is set up, that it does not play a default robot greeting, and that the name on the greeting is yours.

Then look yourself up the way a recruiter will. If the first result is a photo you would not show a manager, that is the version of you they meet before the interview.

6. Not following up, or following up wrong

Silence after an application is normal and it is not a rejection. A short note five to seven working days later, to the person who would manage the role, moves your name from the pile to a conversation more often than people expect.

Three lines: the role, one sentence on why you fit, and a clear ask about timing. No "just checking in," no fourth message. If two follow-ups produce nothing, the answer is no and your energy belongs somewhere else.

7. Accepting before you understand the benefits

A $48,000 offer with a 3% 401(k) match and a subsidized health plan is not the same job as a $48,000 offer with neither. The match alone is $1,440 a year of your own money, matched, and the health premium difference between two plans is routinely $600 to $1,800.

Ask for the benefits summary before you accept, in writing, and read the enrollment window. It is usually thirty days from your start date and it does not reopen because you were busy. What to do with the form when it lands is in the benefits form you fill in during week one.

The mistake underneath all seven: treating the application as paperwork rather than as the first negotiation. Your salary, your start date and your benefits are all set in this window, and none of them can be renegotiated as easily once you have signed. Two hours of care here is worth more than two months of overtime later.

What to do this week

Not all seven. These four, in order, and the rest follow naturally.

  1. Fix the email address and the voicemail greeting. Fifteen minutes, once, forever.
  2. Pick five postings you actually want and tailor the top third of your CV for each. One evening.
  3. Send one short message to a person at two of those companies before you submit.
  4. Decide your salary range now, in writing, so you are not inventing it in a form field at midnight.

When the offer comes, the number you accept becomes the base for every budget on this site. Before you spend it, run it through how to budget your first salary, and put the first month's surplus into saving when your salary is low rather than letting it dissolve.

FAQ

How many applications should I send before I expect interviews?

There is no honest universal number, and anyone quoting one is guessing about your field. The useful rule is about ratio, not volume: if twenty tailored applications produce no interviews, the problem is your CV or your target roles, not your effort. Sending eighty more of the same will produce the same silence.

Should I apply for a job when I do not meet every requirement?

Yes, if you meet the core of it. Postings are wish lists, and most hires do not tick every line. Apply when you can do roughly seventy percent of the day-to-day work and can explain how you would learn the rest. Do not apply when the missing piece is the whole job.

Does a cover letter still matter for a first job?

Less than the top third of your CV, and more than nothing. If the posting asks for one, write four sentences: the role, one specific reason you fit, one thing you have done that proves it, and a close. If it does not ask, a strong tailored CV is enough.

How long should I wait before following up?

Five to seven working days after applying, then once more about a week after that. Two messages total. Anything beyond that reads as pressure rather than interest, and the answer was already no.

Is it bad to take the first offer I get?

Not if you have compared it to the market and read the benefits. It is bad to take it in the same hour it arrives. Ask for the benefits summary, ask one question about the salary range, and give yourself a day. Almost every employer expects that pause, and the ones who do not are telling you something useful.

Your next step

Tonight, before you apply to anything else, do the fifteen-minute version: new email address, voicemail greeting recorded, and your salary range written down where you can see it. Then send three tailored applications this week instead of thirty generic ones.

The application is the cheapest part of the job to get right, and it is the part that decides what the next three years pay.
