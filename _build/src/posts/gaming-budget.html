A new game launched today at $79.99, and the number that actually matters isn't $79.99. It's that $80 is 14 percent of the money you have for fun this month, or about two-thirds of one week's spending. That's the sentence nobody in a game store puts on the shelf.

This isn't a post telling you not to buy games. Games are one of the cheapest forms of entertainment per hour that exist, and a good one costs less per evening than a coffee. It's a post about paying for them in a way that doesn't quietly rearrange your month.

Everything below works whether you're on a console, a laptop, or a phone, and whether you buy one game a year or twelve.

The real cost of a new game

The sticker price is the beginning of the bill, not the end of it. Here's what one big release usually drags behind it, using typical US prices.

The pattern is that the game is roughly half of what a new release actually asks for in the first month. If you're budgeting for the sticker price, you're budgeting for about half the bill.

Where $80 comes from in a real month

Same person as the rest of this site: $48,000 a year, $3,129 take-home, and $563 of that is spending money after the walls and the savings transfer. That's about $130 a week.

So an $80 game is:

  • 14 percent of your entire month's fun money, in one purchase
  • 62 percent of one week's spending, which means that week is a game and a coffee
  • 6.4 percent of your monthly savings transfer, if you borrowed it from there instead

None of those numbers are outrageous. That's the point. A game is affordable on this salary. The problem is never one game, it's three games in a launch window plus a subscription plus a controller, and none of them individually felt like a decision.

Three rules that keep gaming from eating the month

1. Nothing over a third of your fun money. On $563, that's about $188, so a single $80 game clears easily. This rule exists to catch the $300 collector's edition and the console bought on impulse, not to make you feel bad about a normal purchase.

2. Wait 30 days on anything over $40. Not because you shouldn't have it, but because launch-week excitement is a chemical event. If you still want it in a month, buy it, and it will probably be cheaper by then anyway. If you've forgotten about it, you just saved yourself $80 and a little shelf space.

3. Never finance a game. Buy-now-pay-later on a $79.99 purchase turns one decision into four, and a credit card balance for entertainment is the most expensive way to have fun that exists. If the money isn't there this month, the game will still be there next month, on sale.

Launch week is the worst value in gaming

Not the worst experience. The worst value, and those are different things.

  • Day-one patches. The version you play in week one is the least finished version that will ever exist. Servers are overloaded, bugs get fixed, and the first big patch usually lands within a month.
  • Price drops are predictable. Most single-player releases see their first real discount within six to twelve weeks, and a meaningful one within six months. Waiting is not a sacrifice, it's a strategy with a known return.
  • Complete editions arrive later. The version with the expansions bundled in usually costs less than buying the base game plus the season pass at launch.
  • Holiday and summer sales are real. If you can name the next big sale on your platform's calendar, you can name the date you're buying the game at 30 percent off.

Multiplayer is the exception. If your friends are all in a game on launch week, waiting has a social cost that a single-player game doesn't, and that's a legitimate reason to pay full price. Just say that out loud when you decide, instead of calling it a necessity.

The hour test: before buying, guess how many hours you'll actually play. Divide the price by that number. Under $3 an hour is excellent value. Over $10 and you're paying cinema prices for something you might not finish.

Set one monthly games number

The alternative to deciding every time is deciding once. Pick a monthly amount for games, put it in its own line in the budget, and let it roll over.

$40 a month is $480 a year, which is about six full-price games, or a dozen in sales. That's more gaming than most people with a job have time for. $20 a month still buys three or four good games a year if you buy them on sale, which is the entire trick.

Two things to do with that line:

  • Let it accumulate. A month where you buy nothing becomes a month where you can buy a bigger thing without guilt, or a console without a payment plan.
  • Audit the subscriptions once a year. Online subscriptions and game passes are the gaming equivalent of a gym membership. If you haven't used it in two months, cancel it. You can always resubscribe for a month when something you want lands.

The console question

Consoles are the single biggest gaming purchase, and the one most likely to be financed badly. A $499 console plus a game is roughly a fifth of a month's take-home on this salary, so it needs to be a planned purchase, not an emotional one.

Three questions before you buy:

  1. Is there more than one game I want on it? Buying a console for a single release is how a $500 box becomes a $500 dust collector in March.
  2. Do I already own something that plays it? A lot of big releases ship on the previous generation, or on a laptop you already have.
  3. Can I pay for it in one month without touching savings? If not, save for it in a separate pot for three months, and buy it when the money exists. A console is the exact item that a sinking fund was invented for.

Ways to play for less, honestly

  • Your backlog. Most people own four or five games they never finished. That's free entertainment sitting in a library.
  • Sales calendars. Big seasonal sales are predictable. Make a wishlist and buy nothing until the sale.
  • Physical discs, secondhand. Used copies are cheaper, resellable, and finish a game for a fraction of the launch price.
  • Game passes, for a month. Subscribing for one month to play one specific game is often cheaper than buying it, as long as you cancel.
  • Rental services and libraries. Some services still rent games by post, and a few public libraries lend them. Worth one search in your area.
  • Indies. The best value in gaming is consistently a $15 to $25 indie that runs 40 hours, not a $80 blockbuster that runs 12.

What to do about the fear of missing out

The hardest part of waiting isn't the money, it's the two weeks of seeing everyone else play it. Three things make that survivable.

Mute the game's name on social for a month, if your platform allows it. Spoilers are the only thing that waiting actually risks, and they're avoidable. Pick a specific date you're buying it, so waiting feels like a plan instead of a denial. And remember that everyone playing on launch day is playing the buggiest version, which is a genuinely worse experience than the one you'll get in six weeks at 30 percent off.

Buying a game at launch is paying full price to be a beta tester with extra steps.

How this fits the rest of your budget

Games are fun money, not a bill. They sit in the same bucket as dinners, clothes and concerts, which means they compete with those things rather than hiding behind them.

That's why the number is easy to find: it's whatever is left after the four walls and the savings transfer, split across everything you actually enjoy. If gaming is your main hobby, it deserves a bigger share than coffee. If it's a rainy-day thing, it deserves a smaller one. Either way, deciding the share once beats deciding every time a store page tells you it's 20 percent off for the next 48 hours.

The structure for all of it is in the first-salary budget, and the rule about never financing entertainment is one of the ten in the money mistakes post. If you're still working out what your paycheck can actually cover, start with where the deductions go.

FAQ

How much should I budget for games each month?

Between $20 and $40 for most people in their first job, kept as its own line in the fun money. At $40 a month you can buy about six full-price games a year, or a dozen in sales, which is more than most working people finish.

Is it worth buying games at launch?

Only for multiplayer, where your friends are playing, or for a developer you genuinely want to support at full price. For single-player, waiting six to twelve weeks usually means a better version at a lower price, and the game doesn't expire.

Is a console worth it if I only want one game?

Usually not. A $499 console plus one $80 game is a lot of money for one experience. If you can name three games you want on it, the maths starts to work; if you can name one, wait until you can name three.

Should I use buy-now-pay-later for a game or console?

No. It turns one purchase into several payments and makes an optional purchase look like a bill. If the money isn't there this month, save for it, and the price will probably drop while you do.

Are game subscriptions worth the money?

Only if you use them most weeks. Subscribing for one month to play one game and then cancelling is genuinely good value. Paying monthly for a year without opening it is the most expensive way to own games you never play.

How do I stop impulse buying games in sales?

Keep a wishlist and a rule: nothing gets bought outside a planned sale window, and nothing gets bought that isn't on the list. A 60 percent discount on a game you didn't want is still $30 spent.

Your next step

Three numbers, five minutes, and gaming stops being a source of guilt.

  1. Write down your monthly fun money. That's what's left after the four walls and the savings transfer.
  2. Decide the games share, as a number, and put it in the budget as its own line. $20 or $40 are both fine.
  3. Add the games you want to a wishlist instead of a cart, and set a date for the next sale.

Then the next launch day is a decision you already made, not a decision a store page makes for you.
